How to plan an executive committee offsite: a seven-step protocol

By Dr. Sven Mulfinger · Published October 2026

Plan an executive committee offsite backwards from the decisions you need on Monday morning, and choose the venue last. Settle on two or three binding outcomes, interview every participant in confidence beforehand, keep the decision-making group to ten people or fewer and bring in an external facilitator who has run a company, so the CEO can take part as a peer. Leave with one owner per decision, an explicit stop list and review dates at 30, 60 and 90 days.

How an expensive offsite fails

Most executive committee offsites fail in the same ways. The schedule fills up with functional slide updates. The CEO chairs the sessions and, without meaning to, shuts down dissent, because nobody wants to challenge the person who sets their pay. Friction between the commercial and technical leads never comes up in the room and reappears in the corridors and side chats during the breaks.

The team leaves with a sense of harmony and a list of fifty action points, and nobody owns the trade-offs. Within two weeks of being back at the office, day-to-day firefighting has swallowed the lot.

The stakes are real. Scott Keller of McKinsey, drawing on research for Leading Organizations (2017), reports that companies whose top teams work together towards a common vision are 1.9 times more likely to achieve above-median financial performance. Getting there takes a deliberate planning process, more than good event logistics.

The seven steps

1. Define what must be different on Monday morning

Before anyone looks at calendars or flights, answer one question: which decisions, boundaries or habits must change the moment we are back in the office?

Turn the answer into two or three concrete outcomes. For example:

  • Agreeing the top three commercial priorities for the next four quarters, and which initiatives stop.
  • Ending the overlap between Product and Engineering on who decides when a release goes to market.
  • Agreeing an escalation route so that a deadlock on budget is broken within 48 hours.

If an outcome cannot be written as a decision, an owner or an item on the stop list, take it off the agenda.

2. Diagnose the room before you design the agenda

Never build the agenda around what the team says in public meetings. The stated reason for an offsite is usually polite and abstract (“strategic alignment”). The real constraint is usually structural or personal (“two VPs have stopped talking and the roadmap is stuck between them”).

Four to six weeks before the offsite, an independent third party should hold confidential 1:1 interviews of 30 to 45 minutes with every attendee, under mutual NDA. These conversations bring out:

  • The subjects everyone avoids in the weekly meeting.
  • Different readings of what was promised to the board and how much runway there really is.
  • Old debts from reorganisations, missed promotions or broken promises.
  • The handovers between functions where work quietly stalls.

The findings go into an anonymised Leadership Friction Matrix. Personal grievances become patterns in the organisation, which the team can work on in the room without anyone being singled out. How mediation works describes this step in detail.

3. Keep the room small

The quality of decisions drops as the room grows.

Keep the executive offsite for the people who make the decisions: the CEO, the co-founders and the executive committee. Cap it at ten. In the same McKinsey Quarterly article, Keller notes that research suggests a top team's effectiveness starts to diminish once it has more than ten members.

If department heads need to turn the decisions into plans, hold a wider session or a company offsite in the weeks after, instead of crowding the decision-making table. Our guide on offsites, retreats and kick-offs covers that sequence.

4. Replace presentations with pre-reads

No slide updates in the room. Functional reports, financial models and market assessments go out as required reading seven days before. Time together is for debate and trade-offs.

5. Choose a place built for focus

Avoid the business hotel. Conference suites under fluorescent light, a busy lobby and the city outside keep everyone in the habits that created the friction in the first place.

Choose a private buyout somewhere secluded, such as a 500-year-old stone masia in Catalonia or a country estate, with:

  • Complete privacy, with no outside guests, so people can speak freely about the business.
  • Room to move: walks and outdoor sessions, where difficult conversations often go better side by side.
  • Several private breakout rooms for small working groups.

6. Bring in an external facilitator who has run a company

A CEO who facilitates their own offsite cannot fully take part. When the boss holds the pen, the room defers, candour drops and people weigh the political cost of speaking up.

Bring in a facilitator who has carried CEO or board-level P&L responsibility. Someone with that background:

  • Has credibility with demanding executives from the first hour.
  • Steps in when the conversation drifts into operational detail.
  • Holds the tension and names polite evasion without carrying any political risk.
  • Leaves the CEO free to listen, challenge and contribute as a member of the team.

7. Sign the 90-day compact and set the follow-up

Never close an offsite on a wave of goodwill. Before anyone leaves, the commitments go into a one-page 90-day compact that records:

  1. The strategic bets: the outcomes the business will deliver this quarter.
  2. One owner per bet: a named executive accountable for each, with no shared ownership.
  3. The stop list: what the team stops doing straight away to free up capacity.
  4. Review dates: the day 30, 60 and 90 sessions, in everyone's calendar before they leave.

Paul Rogers and Marcia Blenko of Bain showed in Harvard Business Review (“Who Has the D?”, 2006) how unclear decision roles stall decisions and blur accountability. The compact exists to make those roles explicit.

The planning timeline

  • Eight to ten weeks before: agree the outcomes with the CEO, book the venue and the facilitator.
  • Four to six weeks before: hold the confidential 1:1 interviews, build the Friction Matrix and draft the agenda.
  • Two weeks before: send the pre-reads and confirm logistics, dietary needs and transport.
  • The offsite: two to three days of closed-door debate, trade-offs and the signed compact.
  • Days 30, 60 and 90: formal reviews to check progress and hold each owner to account.

Common questions

How far ahead should an executive committee offsite be planned?

Six to ten weeks. That leaves time for confidential 1:1 interviews with every attendee, an anonymised Leadership Friction Matrix and pre-reads that go out a week before arrival.

Why should the CEO not facilitate the offsite?

A CEO who runs the session cannot take part as a peer. People defer to the person who sets their pay, candour drops and the real friction stays in the corridor.

What is the most important output of an executive offsite?

A signed one-page 90-day compact: one accountable owner per decision, a stop list of what the business will no longer do and review dates already in the calendar.
