How this is calculated

The model assumes 46 working weeks a year and a 40-hour working week, which gives 1,840 working hours per leader.

  • Executive payroll = headcount × average annual cost
  • Paid for internal meetings = payroll × (meeting hours ÷ 40)
  • Leadership friction cost = paid for internal meetings × friction factor
  • Sunk executive hours = headcount × meeting hours × 46 × friction factor
  • Full-time leaders equivalent = sunk executive hours ÷ 1,840
  • Time lost per leader = meeting hours × friction factor, shown per week, as a share of 40 hours and as working weeks a year (× 46)
  • Cost per week = leadership friction cost ÷ 46, and per working day = leadership friction cost ÷ 230

The friction factor is your own estimate of how much meeting time adds no decision. It is the input that moves the result most, so test all three presets.

How to read these numbers

The annual cost is a payroll figure. It counts what you pay your leaders for time spent in meetings that re-debate settled calls, repeat status that could be written down or chase consensus. It does not count the cost of decisions that arrive late, which this model does not measure.

Sunk executive time converts the same drag into capacity. A result of one full-time leader means that one senior salary is being spent, in effect, on internal friction alone.

The time lost per leader shows what each person gives up over a working year, in weeks, and what share of an ordinary 40-hour week that is.

Where the drag usually comes from

  • Decisions that are made in one meeting and reopened in the next, because nobody owns the call.
  • Status updates delivered live that could have been read beforehand.
  • Consensus loops, where every function has a vote and nobody holds the decision.

Questions to take into your next leadership meeting

Answer these separately, then compare. The gaps between your answers are where the drag sits.

  1. Which decision from your last leadership meeting was reopened this week, and who reopened it?
  2. Who could say no to your most important current initiative without having said so out loud?
  3. For your three biggest open decisions, can everyone name the single person who owns the call?
  4. Which standing meeting would nobody miss if it stopped next month?
