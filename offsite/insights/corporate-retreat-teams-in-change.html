Corporate interventions for teams in transition: turning uncertainty into operational velocity

By Dr. Sven Mulfinger · Published October 2026

A transition intervention is a focused, partner-led working retreat for a leadership team in the middle of a structural change: a merger, a restructuring, a new CEO or a new operating model. It has two jobs. First, bring out and resolve the unspoken friction and territorial tension the change has created. Second, agree who decides what and sign a 90-day compact before the leadership team takes its priorities to the wider organisation.

Organisations do not absorb change like a software update. Announcing new direction by email or on an all-hands call, before the leadership team itself is aligned, almost always leads to defensive silos, people leaving and slower execution.

What change leaves behind

When a company makes an acquisition, reorganises or changes its business model, the leadership team usually treats it as an analytical and legal exercise. The organisation chart is redrawn, reporting lines are set on paper and synergy targets go to the board.

The odds are poor. In Harvard Business Review (“The New M&A Playbook”, 2011), Clayton Christensen and his co-authors put the failure rate of mergers and acquisitions at between 70% and 90%, pointing to the wrong targets, overpaying and poor integration. In our experience the integration problems that do the most damage are human ones: executives and teams who never worked through what the change cost them.

People carry the memory of past restructurings, colleagues who left, sudden shifts of authority and promises they feel were broken. When nobody addresses this openly and in a neutral setting, it shows up in daily work as quiet resistance:

  • Merged co-founders or VPs agree in the meeting and then tell their legacy teams to protect their budgets and their product turf.
  • Decisions slow down because leaders hesitate to make contentious calls across unfamiliar boundaries.
  • Acquired founders and strong operators lose their sense of agency, disengage and eventually leave.

Compliance alone does not carry a company through change. To get momentum back, the leadership team has to work through the human and governance friction together, in the same room.

Five situations that call for an intervention

Situation Where the friction sits What the intervention does
Post-merger integration Different cultures, overlapping responsibilities and distrust between acquired founders and the parent’s executives Maps the overlap, sets clear decision boundaries and gives every decision one owner
Restructuring and redundancies Survivor syndrome, damaged trust between peers and fear of another round Acknowledges the loss openly, clears the backlog of grievances and resets who does what
A new CEO Old habits meeting new expectations and a different way of communicating Resets the team’s rhythm, clarifies governance and builds candour between peers
A growth step change Informal early-stage habits that stop scaling, with bottlenecks and overloaded executives Moves from informal agreement to written decision rights with one owner each
An AI operating model shift Anxiety about changing roles, scattered tool adoption and uncoordinated technology bets Tests the commercial workflows, removes duplicate initiatives and sets clear technology governance

The four-step protocol

1. Listen before you design

Never build the agenda around what the leadership team assumes the organisation is going through.

Four to six weeks before the offsite, an independent mediator holds confidential 1:1 interviews across the leadership group, under mutual NDA. They bring out:

  • What leaders and their teams are really worried about behind closed doors.
  • Where authority is still unclear or contested between departments.
  • Which decisions have stalled since the change was announced.

The findings go into an anonymised Friction Matrix, so the team can treat resistance as a pattern in the organisation and nobody is put on trial. How mediation works describes this step in detail.

2. The leadership team aligns first

The executive committee has to be clear among itself before it presents anything to middle management or the wider company.

If senior leaders send even subtly different messages about ownership, resources or priorities, cynicism spreads fast. The first sessions take on the hard internal debates:

  • Naming what has been difficult, lost or disrupted.
  • Saying clearly what will stay the same, which gives people something stable to hold on to.
  • Agreeing the direction and the operating model for the next twelve months.

3. Mixed working pods instead of team-building games

Icebreakers and corporate games do little for trust between people who have just been merged or reorganised. Working side by side on a real commercial or operational problem does far more.

The retreat splits participants into mixed pods that cut across the old divides:

  • Product and engineering leads from both sides testing a combined delivery roadmap.
  • Commercial and operations leads working out handovers, service levels and the risks of moving clients across.
  • Each pod guided by a facilitator who has run a business and keeps the debate on execution instead of old grievances.

4. Sign the 90-day transition compact

The intervention ends with something concrete. Each area commits to a one-page compact, based on the 90-day compact and signed by the whole leadership team before anyone leaves:

  • Three deliverables: the milestones each department lead will deliver this quarter.
  • One owner per initiative: exactly one executive with the final say.
  • A stop list: the legacy projects, reporting loops and duplicate processes the combined business ends straight away.
  • Review dates: day 30 and day 60 sessions to check integration progress and clear new bottlenecks.

From compact to company kick-off

Once the leadership team has aligned behind closed doors, the company can come together for a kick-off or company-wide gathering. Our guide to offsites, retreats and kick-offs explains why that order matters.

The kick-off works because the groundwork is done:

  • One story: the CEO and the functional leaders say the same thing from the stage.
  • Few priorities: no more than three strategic bets, simple enough for anyone in the company to repeat.
  • Honest trade-offs: leadership explains what is being dropped, so the new focus is credible.
  • Teams translate the plan: instead of hours of slides, breakout sessions turn the company priorities into team goals and sprint plans.

Why it takes former operators

When a company goes through real change, event organisers and change-management theory rarely produce results that last.

Leading a transition needs facilitators who have carried operating responsibility themselves:

  • Former CEOs and operators who have been through integrations, restructurings and difficult boards.
  • Outside mediators who can hold the tension, name the fears nobody voices and keep the team from retreating into politics.
  • People who keep the human work tied to the commercial results the change was meant to deliver.

Uncertainty is part of any major change. Handled openly, with clear governance, it becomes the starting point for faster execution.

Common questions

Why does announcing change by email or video call fail?

Organisations do not absorb change like a software update. An announcement leaves the anxiety, old grievances and uncertainty about territory untouched. They come back as quiet resistance and slower decisions.

When should a transition intervention take place?

In the first 30 to 60 days after the deal or the restructuring is announced: once the executive committee has set the broad direction but before new bad habits settle in.

How is an operator-led intervention different from team-building?

Team-building relies on games that rarely carry over into the boardroom. An operator-led intervention works on real commercial problems such as overlapping authority and friction between divisions or founders. It ends with a signed 90-day compact.
